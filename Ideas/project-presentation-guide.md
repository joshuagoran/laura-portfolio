# Project Presentation Guide

Recommendations from a landscape architect critique, calibrated for early-career LA reality. Laura is based in Ohio/Brooklyn, early career, with a mix of built work, ongoing projects, academic work, and limited professional photography.

## Core Principle

The site should feel like: "Someone at the beginning of something exciting. Serious training, a distinctive point of view, and the kind of plant knowledge most designers twice her age don't have. The portfolio is young but the thinking is mature."

## Minimum Viable Project Page (4 Requirements)

1. **At least one good image** — photo (iPhone fine), sketch, plan drawing, or rendering
2. **Honest specific description** (3-5 sentences) — what the project was, what Laura did, what plants/materials were involved. In Laura's voice, not generic portfolio-speak.
3. **Context** — built or unbuilt? Academic or professional? What was Laura's specific role? Stated plainly.
4. **Location**

### Nice to Have (add when available)
- Additional images (more photos, process sketches, plan drawings)
- Plant palette with botanical names
- Materials list
- Before/after documentation
- Client brief or site conditions
- Note on how the garden is maturing

### Cut It If
- The only image is blurry, dark, or unrecognizable
- Laura can't write a specific description of what she did
- The project contradicts the kind of work Laura wants to attract

## Project Page Structure (Recommended Order)

1. Hero image (best single photo, sketch, or plan)
2. Title + location (drop year and type labels)
3. The brief/challenge (2-3 sentences, plain language — what the client wanted, site conditions)
4. 2-3 images interspersed with text (NOT all in a grid at the end)
5. The design response (3-5 sentences, name plants and materials)
6. 1-2 more images (before/after, seasonal, detail)
7. Plant palette (5-8 plants, common + botanical names)
8. Materials list
9. Optional: note on garden maturation
10. Navigation to next project (NOT a contact CTA)

## What to Omit

- **Design thesis blockquotes** — "Let the jungle be the architecture" is design-school language. Weave philosophy into the description instead.
- **Year as prominent metadata** — only useful as context ("photographed in its second growing season")
- **Category/type labels** — should be self-evident from the description and photos
- **Collaborators as a prominent section** — demote to small footnote for residential work
- **Generic CTAs on every project page** — keep them browsing, not selling

## Handling Different Project Types

### Built Work (Completed)
- Show the result. Photos (iPhone fine), description of what was done, plant palette, materials.
- State Laura's role clearly: "I developed the planting plan for..." or "I designed the layout and plant palette for..."

### In-Progress / Ongoing
- Use sketch or plan as hero (not construction mud)
- State status clearly: "Currently in construction, expected completion fall 2026"
- Show process images: sketches, site analysis, planting plans
- Construction-phase iPhone photos are fine when framed as in-progress
- Update the page over time as the project progresses

### Limited Role
- Be specific about Laura's contribution: "Planting design for a residential garden designed by [firm]"
- Frame expertise, not limitation: this shows she can be hired specifically for planting design

### Academic / Thesis Work
- Label clearly: "Master's thesis project, University of Cincinnati, 2022"
- Present deliverables as design documents: plans, sections, renderings, analysis diagrams
- Don't pretend it's professional work. Strong academic work with clear thinking is a better portfolio piece than vague professional work.

### Competitions
- Label: "Competition entry for [name], [year]"
- If not selected, don't mention that — just present the design

### Volunteer / Community
- Include them. They show values, community engagement, and real-world experience.
- Often projects where Laura had more autonomy, so the work is more authentically hers.

## Photography Direction

### iPhone Photography Tips
- **Golden hour only** — first and last hour of daylight
- **Eye-level perspective** (5-5.5 feet), looking through the space, not down at it
- **Foreground/midground/background layers** for depth
- **Wait one growing season** before primary documentation if possible

### Ideal Shot List (4-8 photos per project)
1. Hero shot — overall feel, golden hour, eye-level
2. Arrival/threshold — front path, gate, entry
3. Primary gathering space — patio/seating set up for use
4. Planting detail — close-up of key plant combination
5. Material detail — stone joints, wood grain, hardscape edge
6. Long view — from house looking out
7. Before shot (iPhone fine — bad quality is an asset here)
8. Seasonal comparison — same view, different season

### What Kills Landscape Photos
- Overhead drone shots as hero images
- Wide-angle lens distortion (makes small gardens look cheap)
- Overcast midday (flat, washed out)
- Freshly installed landscapes (nursery display look)

## How Many Projects

- 4-6 projects of mixed type and documentation quality, presented honestly
- At this stage, showing range is more important than narrowing to residential
- Mix built and unbuilt work with clear labels — don't separate into tiers that highlight thin "built" section
- Better to have fewer deeply honest projects than more shallow ones

## Content Strategy

### About Page (Most Important Page for Early Career)
- Write in first person
- Tell the story: Akron roots → urban planning → LA → London planting design → herbalism
- The trajectory IS the selling point
- Talk about why she chose this path, what she learned in London, how herbalism changes how she sees plants
- Be direct about location and who she wants to work with
- Don't use "studio" or "firm" if it's just Laura

### Sketches / Process Page (Strategically Vital for Early Career)
- Reframe as "Process" or "Design Thinking"
- Add introductory text about why Laura sketches
- Connect sketches to projects where possible
- For early career, design thinking demos matter more than built work

### Services Page
- Keeping pricing is brave and good — shows she's running a real practice
- Rewrite from SaaS-style cards to editorial prose with process explanation
- Explain what the client gets in tangible terms
- Describe the typical project timeline and process

### Plant Knowledge as Competitive Advantage
- Laura's herbalism + botany training is genuinely unusual
- Name plants in every project description — common + botanical names
- Explain WHY she chose specific plants for specific situations
- This is proof of expertise that most early-career LAs can't match

### Geography
- State clearly: based in Ohio/Brooklyn (or however Laura describes her range)
- Don't be vague to seem bigger — be specific to seem real

## Weekend Documentation Sprint

What Laura can do right now:
1. Inventory all work (school, internship, volunteer, freelance, personal experiments)
2. Write raw descriptions from memory for each project
3. Gather images from phone camera roll, email, files, Instagram, old presentations
4. Visit accessible project sites for 4 quick iPhone shots each (golden hour)
5. Sketch plans from memory for inaccessible projects

## What the PM and Designer Got Wrong

- The "conversion funnel" homepage is wrong for LA — nobody hires on the first visit. Soften the CTA.
- "Editorial restraint" is designer-brained — warmth and specificity matter more than layout polish
- The reference sites (Patel, Future Green) are established-practice models, not early-career models
- They optimized for how the container looks rather than what goes in it
- Content is the bottleneck, not CSS transitions
